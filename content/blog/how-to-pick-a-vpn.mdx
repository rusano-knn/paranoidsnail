# How to pick a VPN without a headache

Every VPN homepage reads like it was written by the same confident stranger. "Double-encrypted,
military-grade, invisible, bulletproof anonymity!" It's all caps and no receipts. If literally
every product claims to be the ultimate shield, then either the rest are lying or one of them
hasn't noticed it's losing.

Here's the problem: a VPN is a genuinely useful tool, and it's hard to buy one well because the
marketing is designed to make it impossible to compare. Everyone says the same thing, so nobody
actually tells you what matters.

Let's fix that. We'll talk first about what a VPN really does and what it quietly does not do.
Then we'll go through a short, honest checklist you can use on any vendor, no technical
background required. Then we'll look at the red flags that should make you close the tab. Spoiler:
a VPN is not a magic cloak, and anyone who tells you it is, is selling you something other than a
VPN.

## What a VPN actually does

Strip away the superlatives and a VPN does two concrete things.

First, it hides your traffic from your internet service provider. Without a VPN, your ISP can see
the sites you visit — the actual domains, the times, the patterns. With one, your device and the
VPN's server wrap that traffic in encryption, so your ISP just sees "this customer is sending
encrypted data to one particular server." It can't tell which sites you're on. For people with
nosy providers, or providers that sell browsing data on the side, that's a real privacy win.

Second, it changes your apparent IP address to the VPN server's. Websites and apps see the server's
location instead of yours. That's how you can make it look like you're in another city or country,
which is useful for getting around geographic restrictions when you're traveling, or reaching
content that's blocked where you are.

That's it. Those two jobs. Everything else a vendor claims is either one of these dressed up, or a
promise they can't keep.

## What it does NOT do

This is the part almost nobody wants to say, because it's less exciting. But it's the part that
keeps you from being disappointed later.

A VPN does not make you anonymous. Your VPN provider still sees your real IP address and what
you're sending, unless it runs a strict no-log network — and even then, you're trusting a company
on its word, backed by whatever audits it's done. You've just moved the person who can see you from
your ISP to a VPN provider. That's a choice with a reason, but it's not the same as disappearing.

A VPN does not protect you from malware. It doesn't scan files, it doesn't stop phishing, and it
will not save you from downloading something bad because a clever page told you to. If you click
the bad link, the VPN is not going to rescue you.

And a VPN does not fix the many other ways you get tracked. Your browser still has fingerprints,
cookies, and login accounts. If you're logged into the same social accounts with or without a VPN,
those services know exactly who you are regardless of your IP. A VPN hides one channel; it doesn't
erase the rest of you.

Holding all of this honestly matters, because it tells you what problem you're actually solving.
If your goal is "don't let my ISP see my browsing," a VPN is great at that. If your goal is "become
invisible," you need a much bigger, more complicated toolkit, and even then, "invisible" isn't on
the menu.

## A plain checklist that actually works

So how do you pick one without drowning in claims? Here's the short list we use, in no particular
order of superiority because they all matter.

**Independent, verifiable logging policy.** "We keep no logs" is a sentence, not a proof. Look for
a published privacy policy that says it in specific terms, and better, a third-party audit from a
firm that actually examined the servers. Audits aren't perfect, but a company that pays for one is
showing its work.

**An anonymous way to sign up.** Some providers let you create an account with just a username and
password, or even an account number instead of an email address. A provider that offers this is
signaling that it genuinely cares about not collecting your identity, because it's giving that
option up voluntarily. It's a strong signal, not a guarantee.

**Open-source clients where you can get them.** If the VPN's own software is open source, anyone
can inspect it for backdoors and sloppy code. That doesn't mean closed-source VPNs are evil. It
just means you have to trust them on faith, and you don't have to run that trust on faith if you
don't want to.

**No surprise "free" business model.** Free anything costs something. A genuinely free VPN either
smells like an ad network, or it's selling your bandwidth, or it's building a profile of you to
sell. If you're not paying for the product, you're very often the product. Cheap paid VPNs are a
totally reasonable choice; "totally free with no catch" is a claim that deserves suspicion.

**Respectable, verifiable ownership.** Someone owns the company, and that someone lives somewhere.
Jurisdiction matters because it shapes what a company can be legally forced to hand over. A few
minutes of searching "who is behind [name] VPN" and "where is it incorporated" tells you more than
any feature list ever will.

## Red flags to walk away from

Some signals should end the conversation right there.

Ads promising "total anonymity" or "military-grade encryption" with no receipts. The phrase
"military grade" mostly means it uses the same encryption everyone uses; it doesn't mean the CIA is
scrutinizing you. Anyone leaning on that phrase is short on real reasons.

Free services that are really ad networks, or that you can't figure out how they make money. If the
business model isn't obvious and the product is free, assume the model is you.

Vague logging claims. No privacy policy, or a policy that says "no logs" in a headline but then
admits to logging timestamps, IPs, and metadata in the fine print. If they won't be specific about
what they keep, that's your answer.

No company info at all. A provider you can't connect to a real person or a real country is a
provider you can't hold accountable, and accountability is most of what you're buying.

## The calm verdict

Here's the honest bottom line: a VPN is one useful layer in a much bigger picture, not a magic
cloak. Buy the boring, transparent one with published audits and a real sign-up policy, the one
that survives that five-point checklist without blinking. Don't buy the one that promises to make
you a ghost.

And before you buy any of them, take a minute to ask the three honest questions from [threat
modeling](/guides/threat-modeling): what are you actually protecting, from whom, and what happens
if you don't. Most people find that a VPN is the right answer to only part of their problem, and
that knowing that ahead of time is worth more than any headline.

A VPN hides your traffic from your ISP and swaps your apparent location. It doesn't erase you. If
you remember just those two simple truths, you'll pick a good one — and you won't be disappointed
by what it can't do.
