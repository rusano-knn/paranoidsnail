# Threat modeling for ordinary people

"Security" sounds exhausting because it sounds like you must defend against *everyone at once*.
You don't. A threat model is just three honest questions. Answer them and the paranoia gets
manageable — and actually useful.

> **Image: A 3-column diagram ("Who am I worried about?", "What could they take?", "How far would
> they go?") with an arrow showing it narrows down to a specific, proportionate plan.** — placeholder.

## The three questions

1. **Who am I worried about?** The lazy teenager in the same coffee shop? The ad network? The
   government? An ex with a grudge? Being specific changes the answer a lot.
2. **What could they take?** Your social media account? Your bank? A photo? Your location history?
3. **How far would they go?** A password guess? A targeted phish? A SIM swap? Waterboarding the
   toaster (they won't — toasters are innocent)?

Answer those and you stop trying to be "perfectly anonymous" (impossible, overkill) and instead
spend effort where it actually pays off.

## A worked example

- **Who:** your internet service provider, and casual ad trackers.
- **What:** your browsing history and profile of your habits.
- **How far:** they passively log, but won't break into your accounts.

Proportionate plan: a **VPN/DNS** to hide browsing from the ISP, a private **browser** default, and
an **ad/tracker blocker**. That's it. You do *not* need Tails or a hardware key for this one.

- **Different case:** you're a journalist or activist in a risky jurisdiction.
  **Who:** an organized, well-resourced adversary. **How far:** very. Now Tails, Tor, hardware keys,
  and careful metadata hygiene are proportionate — but those are rare cases.

## The trap: the paranoia spiral

The fastest way to quit privacy is to demand *perfection* and then feel you've failed. The snail
respects the spiral but will gently flick it. Ship 20 minutes of improvement, feel good, do another
bit later. Good enough beats abandoned.
