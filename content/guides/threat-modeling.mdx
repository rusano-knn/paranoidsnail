# Threat modeling, the uncomplicated way

Let me let you in on a secret: "security" sounds like a full-time job, and that's the problem.

When people hear the word, they picture a person hunched over seven screens, running Tor on a
laptop that's been glued shut, with a tin-foil hat sourced locally and ethically. Most of us look
at that picture, feel tired just thinking about it, and quietly go back to using the same password
for everything. (Nine times, if we're being honest. Or is that just me?)

Here's what nobody tells you, though: that picture is a lie. Security doesn't have to be about
defending against everyone at once. In fact, the moment you try to do that, you've already lost —
because "everyone" is an infinite list, and your energy is not.

The trick is to shrink the problem. There's a name for the skill of shrinking it, and it's called
**threat modeling**. It sounds like something an accountant would do with too much time on their
hands, but really it's just three honest questions you ask yourself. That's it. Answer them
properly and the paranoia becomes something you can hold in your hand instead of something that
lives in your head at 3 a.m.

I should say up front: this is a blog, and I'm not a security professional. I'm someone who cares
about this stuff and wants you to care a little too — but in a way that actually helps you, not one
that just makes you worried. So let's take this slowly. The snail way.

## The three questions

Here they are, in all their unglamorous glory.

### Question one: Who am I worried about?

This one feels silly to answer, but it's the most important, so don't skip it. It's not "everyone."
Everyone isn't a person. Try to name actual people, or actual kinds of people.

Is it the stranger at the coffee shop who might peer over your shoulder? The advertising company
that seems to know an uncomfortable amount about you? A nosy coworker? An ex who knows your
passwords? A government? A company you owe money to? A hacker who doesn't know you exist but will
attack anyone with an old, unprotected device?

Here's the thing about being specific: the answer changes everything downstream. Worrying about the
coffee-shop snoop and worrying about a government are completely different problems with completely
different solutions. If you don't pick, you'll try to solve both at once and do neither well.

### Question two: What could they take?

Once you've got a face (or an entity) in mind, ask what they'd actually want from you. This is
usually a bit deflating, in a good way.

Your saved passwords? Your bank login? Your social media accounts? A private photo? Your text
messages? Your location history? Your address? The embarrassing search you did that one time at
2 a.m.?

Now here's the kicker: most of us, most of the time, are not carrying state secrets. We're carrying
boring lives. That's not a put-down — it's a relief. It means the stakes are usually lower than the
news would have you believe, which is exactly what makes good threat modeling possible.

### Question three: How far would they go?

This is the question people forget, and it's the one that stops the spiral. How much effort would
this person actually spend to get at you?

A casual snoop won't do much more than glance at your screen and move on. An ad network won't go
through your front door — it just wants you to see more of its ads. A random attacker scanning the
internet will try lazy passwords on thousands of accounts and call it a day.

The point is that effort is a resource, and most threat actors spend it like a person budgeting for
groceries. They want the easy win. The person who makes things even slightly annoying to reach is
usually the person who gets left alone. Not because you're unbeatable — because you weren't worth
the effort.

So draw the line. In one of these cases you'll realize nobody is actually coming for you with more
than a passing glance, and you can breathe. It happens to everyone who actually sits down and does
this.

## Now, a worked example — no thinking required

Let me make this concrete, because I hate advice that floats in the air. Grab a real scenario.

Meet — let's call her Maria. Plausibly you, plausibly your neighbor. Maria works from home, shops
online, does the crossword at a café, and has never once thought about a threat model in her life.
She just wants to feel a little less poke-able.

- **Who is she worried about?** Her internet service provider, plus the general cloud of ad
  trackers that follow her around the web like seagulls after a chip.
- **What could they take?** Her browsing history. A rough profile of her habits, her interests,
  where she logs in, what she buys.
- **How far would they go?** Here's the honest part: they log things. Passively. By default. But
  they're not going to break into her accounts or show up at her door. They're lazy and
  commercial, not malicious.

Now watch the magic. Because Maria named her worries, the answer pretty much writes itself. She
doesn't need a full anonymity setup. She needs:

- a **VPN** (or a good encrypted **DNS**) so her connection isn't logged by her ISP,
- a **browser** that isn't the default one that leaks by design,
- and an **ad and tracker blocker**, so the seagulls stop following.

That's it. That's the whole plan. It takes her an afternoon, costs her maybe a few dollars a month,
and covers what she actually loses sleep over.

Notice what she does *not* need: Tails. No anonymity OS, no hardware security keys, no burner phone
in a sock drawer. For Maria's worries, those tools would be like using a fire extinguisher as a
fly swatter. Effective, terrifying, and wildly out of proportion.

Now take the other end of the scale for contrast. Imagine a journalist reporting on something their
government would rather they didn't. The good news is the tooling changes; the same three questions
still drive it. That person's "who" is organized and well-funded. Their "what" is their identity,
location, and sources. Their "how far" is very, very far. For them, Tails and Tor and hardware keys
and careful attention to metadata stop being overkill and become the proportionate, sensible
answer.

Same method. Different answers. That's the whole game — matching the tool to the honest answers,
not to a marketing brochure.

## The honest version: what this is good at, and what it isn't

I've been pretty positive so far, so let me do the balanced thing and talk about the good and the
less-good. Trust nothing that's only one of those.

The good: threat modeling is basically free. It costs you ten minutes and a cup of coffee, and it
turns a vague, scary feeling into a short list of concrete actions. It also stops you from buying a
pile of shiny security gadgets you don't need. That alone pays for itself.

The good also includes this: it's honest. It doesn't promise you "total privacy" or "bulletproof"
anything, because those don't exist. Instead it tells you where your actual weak points are and
lets you pick which ones are worth plugging.

The less-good: it's only as useful as your honesty. If you hand-wave the questions — "oh, I don't
need to think about that" — you'll produce a plan that covers nothing. If you lie and say the
government is after you when really you're just worried about an ad network, you'll buy Tails and
fear your own shoelaces for no reason, and none of it helps the actual problem.

The other less-good thing: threat modeling doesn't protect you from the person standing behind you.
No framework fixes a password shared with the wrong person, scammed you into giving away, or a
five-second shoulder-surf. These tools handle the far-away threats. The close-up, human-shaped ones
are a different problem, and it's worth knowing the difference.

So use it for what it's for, and don't be embarrassed by what it's not. It's a compass, not a
forcefield.

## The trap, and how to slip it

There's one trap you should see coming from a mile away, because it catches almost everyone,
including me: the demand for perfection.

Here's how it goes. You set up the VPN, you block the trackers, you feel great. Then you hear about
one more thing — maybe a clever fingerprinting trick, maybe a new exploit — and you think: "if I
can't stop that, what was the point of any of this?" The next week you're comparing anonymizing
operating systems at midnight and feeling guilty about your router.

That's the paranoia spiral. And it's the fastest way to quit privacy forever, because you can never
be perfect, and "never perfect" turns into "why bother." It's a loop designed to end in exhaustion,
not in safety.

The snail's view on this — and I'm partial, obviously — is that the spiral isn't worth respecting
enough to climb into. Perfection isn't a destination you can pack for, so don't plan that trip.
Instead, do something smaller and measurable, feel good about it, and come back later for the next
small thing. Twenty minutes of real improvement, on purpose, does more for you than a year of
guilt about the twenty minutes you didn't do.

Good enough is a real, respectable finish line. "Abandoned because I couldn't be perfect" is not a
finish line at all. Choose the first one. Your future self, drinking its coffee and not panicking,
will thank you.

## Where to go from here

You don't need to become an expert today. You just need the three questions in your pocket, and a
plan as small as your honest answers suggest. For most of us, that plan is a VPN, a decent browser,
and a blocker — nothing that will make your grandkids laugh at you.

If you want the concrete first steps, the [Privacy Starter Kit](/guides/privacy-starter-kit) walks
you through the basics in a sensible order. And when you're ready to start choosing tools rather
than just hearing about them, the [tools list](/tools) is a good place to browse with your new,
shrunken problem in hand.

Go slow. Keep it small. And for heaven's sake, stop trying to defend against everyone — you've got
better things to drink your coffee over.
