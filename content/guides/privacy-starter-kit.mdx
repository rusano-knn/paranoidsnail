# The Privacy Starter Kit

You don't need a weekend and a tinfoil hat. You need about twenty minutes and the willingness to
change three defaults. That's it. Most people are leakier because of *habits*, not *threats* — and
habits are easy to fix.

## TL;DR

- Turn off the obvious leaks in your browser and phone settings.
- Get a password manager and put every password in it. Today.
- Stop letting "your phone number" be the master key to your accounts.

## Prereqs

- Nothing. No account to create, no software you must buy.

## Step 1 — Change three defaults

1. **Browser:** switch your default search engine off anything that tracks you. Use DuckDuckGo or
   Startpage on your existing browser, or just use Tor Browser when you want the strong stuff.
2. **Phone:** turn off "personalized ads" and location history. On a phone in our inventory, these
   are buried in Settings → Privacy. Boring, but it stops the biggest obvious drain.
3. **Apps:** revoke permissions for apps that don't need them (camera, mic, contacts, location).
   Ask: *why does a flashlight app need my location?* The answer is never "so it can shine better."

> **Image: A phone Settings panel screenshot with the "Personalized ads" and "Location history"
> toggles highlighted in red boxes with short annotations.** — placeholder.

## Step 2 — Get a password manager

Passwords are the single thing holding your entire digital life together, and most people reuse one
or two everywhere. That's like using the same house key for your door, your car, and your bank.

1. Pick one: **Bitwarden** (easy, generous free tier) or **KeePassXC** (local-first, full control).
2. Generate a long, random master password. Write it on paper and keep it somewhere safe — don't
   save it in your browser in plain text.
3. Move your important accounts into it. Let it generate unique passwords for the rest.

> **Image: A screenshot of Bitwarden's password generator generating a long random password, with an
> arrow to "saved in vault" and a note "Vault is encrypted."** — placeholder.

## Step 3 — Stop using your phone number as identity

Your phone number is effectively public, portable, and passed around by carriers. When it's your 2FA
backup and your password reset, anyone who steals the number can try to steal the accounts.

- Prefer an **authenticator app** (2FA codes live on your device) over SMS codes where an app is
  allowed.
- Use an email address for account recovery that you actually control, not SMS alone.

> **Image: A small side-by-side comparison: "SMS code (can be SIM-swapped)" vs "Authenticator app
> (stays on your device)" with a clearly-labeled tradeoff note.** — placeholder.

## Which tool

- Want the least effort? **Bitwarden + a good browser default + 2FA app.**
- Want more control? **KeePassXC + Tor Browser + a hardware/2FA app.**

## Good / bad tradeoffs

- A password manager is a *single point of failure if you forget the master password* — but that's
  still dramatically better than reusing passwords.
- Phone-number-as-identity is convenient; giving it up is mildly inconvenient but much safer.
