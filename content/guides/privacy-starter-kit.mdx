# The 20-minute privacy starter kit

Here is the thing about privacy advice on the internet: it is usually written for people who
already know what an ISP is, who can draw you a diagram of how Tor works, and who think a weekend
spent configuring your router sounds like a good time.

This guide is not that. This guide is for the person who still has the default browser their
laptop came with, who is not about to reinstall their operating system, and who has maybe thirty
minutes before dinner.

You do not need to become anonymous. You do not need to disappear. You just need to make yourself
a harder, less interesting target. These three things do exactly that. You can do them in about
twenty minutes, and you can undo all of them in about two if you ever feel like it. That is the
whole deal: low effort, easy to undo, quietly effective.

## A note before you start

None of this is a magic shield. A person determined to find you, or a government that wants their
data, is a different problem. These steps are for the everyday stuff: the ads that follow you
around, the sites that watch what you click, and the account you would be very sad to lose. That
is a real and worthwhile improvement, and it's worth doing even though it's not perfect.

If at any step something feels like too much, skip it. Each step works on its own. Doing one is
better than doing none.

## Step 1: Switch your DNS (five minutes)

DNS is the thing that turns a name like `example.com` into the actual address of the website. By
default, your internet provider runs this for you, which means, in plain terms: your internet
provider can see a list of every website you visit. Every one.

You can change who does this job. It is a small setting that most people never touch, and it takes
less than five minutes once you know where to look.

**The good:** you stop handing your internet provider a complete list of everywhere you go online.
You also get protection against "site not found" links that are actually traps — the good DNS
providers block known bad addresses before your browser even reaches them. And you can do exactly
the same thing on your phone, so you get the benefit everywhere.

**The bad:** your new DNS provider now plays the role your internet provider used to play. You are
trusting someone else instead — so you should pick someone whose whole business is privacy, not
someone who makes money from your data. That is the trade, plain and simple: you are choosing who
you trust, and you should choose carefully.

### Where to point it

- **Cloudflare** (`1.1.1.2` / `1.0.0.2`) — fast, and the malware-blocking version specifically
  stops known bad sites. Their business is not built on selling your browsing history.
- **AdGuard DNS** — fast and focuses on blocking ads and trackers at the network level, so even
  apps that don't respect your ad blocker can't call home about you.
- **Quad9** (`9.9.9.9`) — run by a foundation, blocks known malware and phishing domains, and has
  a strict no-logging stance.

You really cannot go far wrong with any of the three. Pick the one that sounds fine to you and
move on — switching later is easy.

### How to actually do it

Look up "change DNS" plus the name of your computer's operating system (Windows, macOS, or your
phone's brand). Each one is a slightly different screen, but they all follow the same shape: find
your network settings, find the DNS box, and type in the numbers above. Step-by-step guides exist
for every device in minutes.

If this feels like the one step you'd rather skip, skip it. Steps two and three are just as
valuable and are even easier.

## Step 2: Install an ad blocker (five minutes)

You have probably noticed that some websites are impossible to read because there is an ad
covering half the article, or a video that starts playing the moment the page opens. Those ads are
not just annoying — they are also watching you. Every ad network on a page is a little camera, and
the page loaded all of them just by you visiting.

An ad blocker stops most of that noise and most of that watching, before it ever reaches you. It
is a small program that runs inside your browser.

**The good:** fewer ads, faster pages, less tracking. It genuinely does cut down how much of your
browsing gets collected and sold. It also makes the web readable again, which is honestly worth it
on its own.

**The bad:** some sites quietly prefer blockers don't exist, and a small number will nag you or
refuse to show content until you turn it off. That is their choice. Most of the internet just
works, and you can always flip the blocker off for one site if you need to. The power stays with
you.

### Which blocker to use

- **If you use Chrome** — install **AdGuard MV3**. Chrome changed its rules and moved away from the
  classic blockers, and AdGuard's MV3 version is the one that still works properly there.
- **If you use anything else — Brave, Firefox, Edge, or similar** — install **uBlock Origin**. It
  is the long-time favorite, it's effective, and it stays out of your way.

That is the entire decision. Chrome → AdGuard MV3. Everything else → uBlock Origin.

## Step 3: Turn on two-factor authentication (ten minutes)

People reuse passwords. It is a fact of life. If a website you signed up for years ago leaks your
password — and yes, that happens all the time — the person who gets it will try that same password
on your email, your bank, your social media. This is how most accounts get broken into. It is not
some master hacker; it is one leaked password reused everywhere.

Two-factor authentication is the fix for this, and it is the single most valuable thing on this
list.

The idea is simple: to get in, you need the password *and* a second thing. Usually that second
thing is a code sent to your phone, or a little number that appears in an app on your phone. Even
if someone steals your password, they cannot get in without the second thing, and the second thing
is on your phone, not on them.

**The good:** it is the biggest single improvement in account safety you can make. It closes the
door on the most common kind of break-in. And "something is better than nothing" really applies
here — even the most basic option is a huge step up from no protection at all.

**The bad:** it is a little bit more inconvenient. You have to have your phone nearby when you log
in. And if you lose your phone without having backups set up, you can lock yourself out — which is
exactly why you should set up a backup method (like printed backup codes) when you turn it on.
Those discomforts are small compared to the account you won't lose.

### Where to turn it on

Start with the account that matters most to you — for most people that's email, because your email
often controls password resets for everything else. Then do your bank, then your social media.
You do not have to do every site tonight. Do the ones that would hurt. Most big sites have a
"Security" or "Password and security" page where this lives, and once you know the shape of it,
it's the same everywhere.

## That's it

Three things. DNS so your internet provider stops getting a full map of your day. An ad blocker so
the ads stop following you around. Two-factor auth so a leaked password stops being a stolen
account.

Do them at your own pace. Do one, stop, come back for the next tomorrow. The version of you that
does just one of these is already ahead of most people, and the snail approves of slow, steady,
deliberate progress — it's practically the brand.

If you want to go further, the door is right there: get a [password manager](/guides/password-manager),
or learn [what your browser's incognito mode does and does not do](/guides/incognito-myth).
But don't feel you have to. Small and steady is how this works.
