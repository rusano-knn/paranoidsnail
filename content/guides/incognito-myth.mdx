# Incognito mode, honestly explained

Every browser has it. A button, a menu item, a little dark window that opens with some version of
"you're now in private mode." It feels important. It feels like the browser is winking at you, like
you two are in on a secret together.

Here's the honest thing: incognito mode is a useful little feature, and it is not a privacy shield.
Those are two different things, and a lot of people (fairly) mix them up. This guide is here to sort
that out, gently, with no tech talk and no scolding. If you've been using incognito and hoping it
was hiding you, you haven't done anything wrong. You were just sold a friendlier story than the
browser actually delivers.

## What incognito actually does

Let's start with the truth, because the truth is genuinely useful.

When you use a normal browsing window, your browser keeps a running record of what you do. It
remembers the sites you visited, the things you typed into forms, the little login cookies that keep
you signed in, the search terms you used. That record lives on your device. Someone could sit down
at your computer afterward, open the history, and read most of your afternoon back to you.

Incognito changes that one thing. While the window is open, the browser does not save your history
or your cookies or your form entries to the device. When you close the window, what happened inside
it is mostly gone from that machine.

That's the whole job. That's the only job. And it's a real job, worth having. But notice what it
isn't: it isn't hiding you from the internet. It's hiding your trail from *your own computer* — and
from the person who might sit down at your computer next.

Think of it like using a library's public computer to look something up. When you stand up and walk
away, that machine doesn't hold a scrap of what you did. That's incognito. It does not make you
invisible to the librarian or the library. It just means the machine forgets you.

## What it does NOT do

This is the part nobody likes hearing, but it's the part that matters.

**It does not hide you from websites.** When you visit a site, the site sees you arrive. It sees
your internet address, your device, your browser. It can track you. It could recognize you again if
you came back. Incognito doesn't turn any of that off. The site just doesn't know that your browser
forgot you — in fact, from the site's side, nothing about you changed at all.

**It does not hide you from your internet provider.** The company that sells you internet can still
see the traffic flowing through their network. So can your employer, if you're using a work
computer, or the owner of a Wi-Fi network you're on. Incognito does nothing here. The data still
passes right by them.

**It does not stop tracking or ads.** Those little bits of code that follow you across the web and
build a profile of your interests? They still work in incognito. A website can still read your
screen size, your language, your plugins, all those small details that add up to a fingerprint as
recognizable as a real one. Incognito doesn't scrub any of it.

So let's put it plainly: incognito hides your history on your own device. That is the complete list.
Everything else you might have hoped it does, it doesn't.

## When it IS genuinely useful

Now the good news, because we shouldn't throw the feature out. There are things incognito is truly
the right tool for.

**A shared or borrowed computer.** You're at a friend's house, or a hotel lobby machine, or the
office, and you want to check your email without leaving your inbox open for the next person.
Incognito is perfect for that.

**A second login.** Many sites only let you be signed in as one person at a time — or they just make
it awkward to switch. Incognito lets you check a second account without logging out of the first.

**Seeing your own site the way strangers do.** If you run a website or a shop, incognito lets you
look at your own page without your own cached login and preferences coloring the view. That's
genuinely handy.

**A cleaner, short-lived session.** Maybe you just don't want a dozen tabs of recipe searches slowly
cluttering your history for years. That's a fine, honest reason to use incognito.

None of these are shameful. These are exactly the things incognito was designed for. Use it for
these. Just know what you're buying.

## What to use instead, for real privacy

If your goal is actually to hide your browsing from the prying eyes around you — your provider, the
ad trackers, the sites themselves — incognito won't get you there. Here's what actually would.

**A VPN** hides the traffic from your internet provider. The provider sees a single encrypted stream
and not much else. That covers one of incognito's blind spots. Mullvad and a few other honest VPNs
do this well and keep no logs.

**A privacy-focused browser and a tracker blocker** take care of the advertising side. They block
the bits of code that follow you and shrink the fingerprint sites can read. Firefox, Brave, and
DuckDuckGo's browser all do a decent job out of the box.

**A private search engine** means your searches don't get filed into a profile about you. DuckDuckGo
and Startpage are the usual names here.

**Tor Browser** is the serious answer for real anonymity — the kind where you genuinely don't want
anyone able to tell where you're browsing from. It routes your traffic through a chain of relays so
that no single point — not the site, not your provider — sees the whole picture. Tor is powerful,
and for most people it's more than they need on a daily basis. It's there when you want it.

The short version: incognito is about you and your own device. VPNs, blockers, private search, and
Tor are about the rest of the internet. Different tools, different jobs. No single one does the
other's work.

## The honest takeaway

Incognito mode is a convenience, not a security tool. It keeps your browsing off your own device for
a session, and that is genuinely all it promises. Use it for the shared computer, the second login,
the quick look you don't want lingering. That's fine. Enjoy it.

Just don't ask it to hide you from the internet, because it can't, and no amount of hoping will
change that. If you need the bigger privacy, there are better tools for it, and they're not hard to
find.

## The good and the bad

Let's close with a straight tradeoff, because a tool this simple deserves one.

**The good.** It's free, it's built into every browser, and it takes one click. For the things it's
made for — a shared computer, a second account, a clean slate — it works perfectly. Very few privacy
tools are that easy.

**The bad.** It's easy to mistake for something much bigger than it is. The name and the marketing
make it sound like privacy, and it isn't. If you take incognito as your real privacy plan, you'll
end up unprotected without knowing it. That's the honest cost: not that the feature is bad, but that
it's easy to over-trust.

So there it is. Incognito is a nice, honest little tool with a slightly dishonest name. Keep it in
your pocket for the things it does. Reach for a VPN, a blocker, and a private search engine when you
actually want to disappear. You don't need to be paranoid to get this right — you just need to know
what's what. That's the whole trick, and the snail is happy to walk you through it slowly.
